Chapter 1

About PSYC3085

PSYC3085 is a 30-credit, year-long module. You plan and carry out an independent empirical study that tests one or more hypotheses, then write it up as a journal article.

30credits, running across both semesters
20%Project proposal, due Wed 2 Dec 2026, 4pm
80%Research paper, due Thu 29 Apr 2027, 4pm

Learning objectives

By the end of the module you should be able to:

  • conduct a comprehensive, critical literature review
  • develop at least one novel, meaningful, informed and testable hypothesis or research question, and choose a suitable design and method
  • demonstrate practical research skills
  • carry out suitable statistical analyses
  • interpret and critically discuss your findings
  • communicate your findings in a full research paper

Assessment 1: Project proposal (20%)

Due Wednesday 2 December 2026, 4pm (UK time). Use the module template. It has three parts:

  • 500 words: background and rationale, literature review.
  • 500 words: study aims and hypotheses, and methodology.
  • 250 words: a reflection on the ethical challenges of your project.

Your supervisor marks the proposal and gives written feedback to use in your research paper.

Assessment 2: Research paper (80%)

Due Thursday 29 April 2027, 4pm (UK time). It's a 5,000-word paper following the author guidelines of the Journal of Southampton School of Psychology (JSSP). A template is provided. The paper is double-marked.

Feedback you'll get

  • Proposal: verbal feedback in supervision meetings, and answers to questions by email before you submit. Supervisors aim to reply within 3–4 working days.
  • Research paper: formative feedback on one full draft. Introduction, Methods and Results each get one high-level comment on strengths and weaknesses, plus two brief comments per page. The Discussion gets one high-level comment.

Chapter 2

How supervision works

We meet as a group every other week in term time, for 30–60 minutes. Individual meetings are available on request.

What happens in a group meeting

  1. Teach10–20 minYour supervisor covers that meeting's topic, usually live in the ESS documentation or your analysis software.
  2. Round-robin2–3 min eachEach of you says what you've done, what's next and where you're stuck.
  3. Clinicrest of the timeThe group works through one or two people's problems. Everyone learns from these.
  4. Next stepslast few minWe confirm the to-do list for the next meeting, which is on the meeting's page here.

We'll agree a regular day and time in Meeting 1.

Individual meetings

Individual meetings are available on request via email: s.leach@soton.ac.uk.

Working together, working independently

You share a dataset and the teaching, and you're encouraged to talk ideas through with each other. But your research question, analysis syntax and writing must be your own. Don't share syntax files or drafts.

Chapter 3

Your dataset: the European Social Survey

The ESS is a cross-national survey that has run every two years since 2002. The most recent round is ESS Round 11, fielded in 2023. You can use whichever round, or rounds, best suit your question.

30countries in the Round 11 integrated file, including the UK
~200core questions, asked in every round
2rotating modules: gender in Europe and inequalities in health

What's in Round 11

Each round has a core questionnaire plus two rotating modules, which change from round to round. The Round 11 core covers topics including:

  • health and wellbeing
  • trust in people and institutions
  • human values
  • immigration, discrimination and identity
  • climate change
  • religion, politics and political participation
  • media use
  • crime and justice
  • sociodemographics: education, work, income, household

The two rotating modules are Gender in contemporary Europe (feminine and masculine identities, sexism, gender discrimination and equality) and Social inequalities in health and their determinants. Earlier rounds repeat most of the core questions but have different rotating modules.

The ESS11 main questionnaire is a good place to start. To explore the questions more easily, including those from other rounds, browse them by theme in the ESS Data Portal.

Access and ethics

Ethics: blanket approval Our group is covered by a blanket ethics approval to use the ESS, so you don't need to submit your own ERGO application.

The data are free for non-commercial use and can be downloaded from the ESS Data Portal after a short registration. When you download, record the edition number of the file, because you'll need it to cite the data.

Building a variable map

A variable map links each idea in your research question to the exact ESS item that measures it. You'll build one in Meeting 2.

ConstructESS variableQuestion (short)ScaleAsked of
Life satisfactionstflifeHow satisfied with life as a whole nowadays0–10Everyone
Social trustppltrstMost people can be trusted, or you can't be too careful0–10Everyone

These are example rows. Check every name, wording and scale against the documentation for the round you use.

Watch out for items that were asked only in some countries, or only of some people (for example, only those in paid work). Also watch for single items standing in for complex constructs, and for subgroups too small to analyse.

Three things to know about ESS data

1. Missing values

Refusals, "Don't know" and "No answer" have their own codes. For 0–10 items these are usually 77, 88 and 99. If you leave them in, they'll be treated as real scores. Check each variable in the documentation and set these codes as missing.

2. Weights

The ESS data file includes survey weights. They adjust for how each country's sample was drawn and for who didn't respond, so that figures such as a country's average life satisfaction match its whole population. You don't need them in this project, for two reasons:

  • Your project tests relationships between variables, such as whether trust predicts wellbeing. Weights matter most when you estimate population averages, and they usually change relationships between variables only a little.
  • In SPSS, Weight Cases treats a weight as a count of people. With survey weights, that gives misleading standard errors and p-values. Handling them properly needs specialist tools that go beyond this project.

Analyse the data unweighted, and say so in one sentence in your Methods.

3. Syntax

Do everything through syntax, using SPSS Paste, an R script or a JASP file, and add comments as you go. This makes your results reproducible, makes your Methods easy to write, and makes the end-of-project data handover simple. Keep an untouched copy of the raw download.

What you don't need to worry about

These don't apply to our group, so you can set them aside.

  • ERGO approvalOur ESS projects are covered by a blanket ethics approval, so you don't submit your own ERGO application. You still write the 250-word ethics reflection in your proposal.
  • Pre-registrationYou don't need to pre-register your study.
  • Applying weightsYou don't need to apply the ESS survey weights in your analyses.

Meeting 2

From interest to a researchable question

w/c 12 Oct 2026Autumn week 445–60 min

You've browsed the questionnaire and have a shortlist of interests. Now we turn them into questions the ESS can actually answer.

Goals

  • Turn a broad interest into a question the ESS can answer.
  • Link each construct in your question to specific ESS variables.
  • Spot feasibility problems before you've invested in an idea.

Bring with you

  • Two candidate ideas, and the ESS questions that could measure them (question numbers and wording).

In the meeting

  • Pitches (2–3 min each): your two ideas, how you'd measure them and why they matter.
  • What the ESS can answer: associations between variables, differences between groups, moderation by demographics or country, and change across rounds.
  • What it can't answer: causal or experimental questions, or anything about constructs it doesn't measure.
  • Mapping constructs to variables: from a questionnaire item to its variable name, response scale and who was asked (Chapter 3).
  • Feasibility traps: items asked only in some countries or of some people, single items for complex ideas, and tiny subgroups.
  • A quick check against the FINER criteria: is the question feasible, interesting, novel, ethical and relevant?

Meeting 3

Literature review and finding the gap

w/c 26 Oct 2026Autumn week 645–60 min

You have a provisional question. Now you find out what's already known, so your study adds something new.

Goals

  • Search the literature systematically, including earlier studies that used ESS data.
  • Turn a pile of papers into an argument that leads to your question.
  • Refine your question so it fills a real gap.

Bring with you

  • Your research question and variable map.
  • The 2–3 papers that matter most to your question.

In the meeting

  • One minute each: your question and your key papers.
  • Search strategy: database search strings (e.g., in PsycINFO or Web of Science). Also search "European Social Survey" together with your construct to see what's been done with these data.
  • From summary to argument: start with the broad problem, then what's known, then the gap, then your study's aim.
  • A synthesis table: for each paper, the sample, measures, findings and limitations.
  • Where novelty comes from with ESS data:
    • the newest round
    • a new pairing of predictor and outcome
    • a new moderator
    • a comparison across countries
    • a psychological theory tested at population scale

To do before Meeting 4

By Meeting 4A refined question and draft hypotheses.

Meeting 4

Hypotheses and analysis plan

w/c 9 Nov 2026Autumn week 860 min

This is where your proposal's Methods section takes shape. The decisions you make here go straight into your proposal.

Goals

  • Write hypotheses that are directional, testable and grounded in the literature.
  • Match each hypothesis to the right statistical test.
  • Decide your sample and covariates in advance.

Bring with you

  • Your draft hypotheses.
  • Your synthesis table and your 500-word background outline.

In the meeting

  • Hypothesis check: is each one directional, testable with ESS variables and grounded in the literature?
  • Matching tests to hypotheses: correlation, t-test or ANOVA, multiple regression, moderation with an interaction term, or logistic regression for yes/no outcomes.
  • Sample: one country, a set of countries, or all countries with country as a control. Decide age limits and exclusions now.
  • Covariates: chosen in advance, each with a reason, for example age, gender, education or income.
  • Large samples: almost every effect will be statistically significant. So decide which effect size would matter, and plan to report confidence intervals.

Meeting 5

Proposal and ethics clinic

w/c 23 Nov 2026Autumn week 1060 minProposal due Wed 2 Dec

This is your last group meeting before the proposal deadline, and the last one of the autumn term. We use it for feedback on your proposal and its ethics reflection, and to plan your work over the break.

Goals

  • Get verbal feedback on your proposal's aims, hypotheses and methods.
  • Understand the ethical issues in analysing secondary survey data.
  • Leave with a plan for handling the data and writing over the Christmas break.

Bring with you

  • Your full proposal draft, including the 250-word ethics reflection.

In the meeting

  • Proposal feedback: 5 minutes each on aims, hypotheses and methods, plus questions from the group.
  • Ethics in this group: our ESS projects are covered by a blanket approval, so there's no ERGO application to write. You still write the 250-word ethics reflection.
  • Material for your ethics reflection:
    • re-identification risk in small subgroups, even in anonymised data
    • the ESS conditions of use: non-commercial use, and citing the data correctly
    • storing data securely on university systems
    • respondents consented to take part in the survey, not to your specific question
    • reporting differences between groups (e.g., gender, ethnicity, migration) without stigmatising anyone
  • Handling the data: download the data for the ESS round or rounds you're using, and note the edition number. Store it on university systems, keep an untouched copy of the raw download, and work in syntax from day one (Chapter 3).
  • Your Christmas writing plan.

Meeting 6

Getting the data ready

w/c 25 Jan 2027Spring week 160 min

The spring term starts with the data. Careful preparation now saves you from redoing analyses later.

Goals

  • Produce a clean analysis dataset from your ESS data file.
  • Describe your sample and variables.
  • Record every decision you make along the way.

Bring with you

  • The ESS data file you downloaded, and its edition number.
  • Your syntax skeleton and your literature review draft.

In the meeting

  • Check your setup: the raw file stored safely, your syntax file started, and missing-value codes understood (Chapter 3).
  • Preparation steps:
    • select your cases, such as countries and age range
    • set missing-value codes to missing
    • reverse-score items where needed
    • compute composite scores and check their reliability (alpha or omega)
  • Descriptives: N, means, SDs, frequencies and distributions.
  • A decision log: every exclusion and recode, with the reason. This feeds your Methods and supplementary materials.

Meeting 8

Writing Methods and Results

w/c 22 Feb 2027Spring week 545–60 min

You have results. Now you write them up clearly, and describe the ESS well enough that a reader could repeat your analysis.

Goals

  • Write a Methods section that describes a secondary dataset properly.
  • Report your results in APA style, hypothesis by hypothesis.
  • Make clear tables and figures.

Bring with you

  • Your analysis output and your one-line summaries of each hypothesis.

In the meeting

  • Methods for secondary data:
    • the ESS design: sampling, fieldwork mode and country coverage
    • your analytic sample and exclusions
    • your measures, with item wording and response scales
    • your analysis plan
  • Citing the dataset: as the ESS requires, including the edition number.
  • Results in APA style: descriptives first, then each hypothesis in order, with one clear table or figure per key finding.
  • Departures from the plan: report them openly, with the reasons.
  • Formatting: the JSSP author guidelines and the module template.

Meeting 9

Discussion and assembling the draft

w/c 8 Mar 2027Spring week 745–60 minFull draft due Fri 19 Mar

The last big section to write. Then you put the whole paper together for feedback.

Goals

  • Structure a Discussion that interprets your findings, rather than repeating them.
  • Name the limitations that come with ESS data.
  • Assemble a complete draft within the word limit.

Bring with you

  • Your Methods and Results drafts.
  • Notes on what your findings mean.

In the meeting

  • Discussion structure: main findings, links back to the literature, implications, limitations, future research and conclusion.
  • ESS-specific limitations:
    • cross-sectional data, so no causal claims
    • self-report and single-item measures
    • whether translated items mean the same across countries
    • non-response
    • constructs the ESS doesn't measure
  • Word budget: 5,000 words. The abstract, references and supplementary materials don't count.
  • What feedback covers: one high-level comment per section and two brief comments per page, with no editing. That's why the draft you send should be as complete as you can make it.
